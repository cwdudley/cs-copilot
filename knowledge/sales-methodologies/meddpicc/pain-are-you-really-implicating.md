# Are You Really Implicating the Pain?

## Muddy puddle vs. leaky ceiling

Borrowing an idea from James Clear: some problems are best left alone (a muddy
puddle dries up), while others compound if ignored (a leaky ceiling eventually
collapses). Sales conversations need to establish which kind of problem this is.

## i1 → i2 → i3, with the ceiling example

| Level | What the seller does | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **i1 Identify** | Recognize the problem exists | Pointing out there is a leak |
| **i2 Indicate** | Show the likely consequence if untreated, ideally quantified | Left alone it collapses, and repair costs far more than fixing it now |
| **i3 Implicate** | Connect the consequence to *their* situation | What does that do to their budget? Which of their projects get delayed? |

## The common failure

Many sellers stop at i1 and believe discovery is done. Identified pain rarely
creates enough urgency. Getting to i3 reduces inertia and improves close rates.

## Pain is fluid

Implications fade from the customer's mind over time. Strong discovery early in
the cycle does not keep urgency alive — implications have to be reinforced
throughout.

## Pipeline self-check

For each open opportunity: is the pain at i1, i2 or i3? Anything below i3 needs
more work before it can be relied on.

## Account-management relevance (model note)

The fading-implication point is especially relevant post-sale: once a solution is working,
the original pain is forgotten, and with it the reason to renew.
