# The Operating Model for Recurring Revenue — SPICED as First-Party Definitions

This research paper was the most detailed **public** Winning by Design statement
of SPICED's element definitions and conversation structure found during
retrieval.

## Six models of recurring revenue

1. Business model
2. Data model
3. Mathematical model
4. Go-to-market model
5. Growth model
6. **Operating model** — where SPICED sits

## Core principles

- **Recurring revenue is the result of recurring impact.**
- Most customer lifetime value comes **after** the initial sale, yet leadership
  often underinvests in onboarding, renewal and growth motions.
- Sales, Marketing and Customer Success need one operating model, framework,
  metric set and language.

## Customer-centric stage language

Rather than prospects, MQLs and qualified opportunities, describe stages from the
customer's side: expressing interest → engaged → committed to a solution → ready
to activate → **achieving recurring impact** → **growing toward maximum impact**.

## Measure post-sale stages granularly

- Split churn into **onboarding churn** and **usage churn** — they behave very
  differently.
- Split growth into **renewal rate** (same decision-makers), **resell rate** (new
  decision-makers), **upsell rate** and **cross-sell rate**.

Improvements in post-sale stages compound, which is why the paper argues for more
detailed Customer Success metrics than most companies track. Most growth in a
healthy recurring-revenue business comes from existing customers moving through
renewal, cross-sell and upsell.

## Why sales methodologies break down

1. **Too many at once.** BANT for prospecting, something else for discovery — none
   of them integrated, which confuses Sales, Marketing and Customer Success.
2. **Solution vs. consultative vs. provocative is a false choice.** Real
   conversations need all three, and methodologies rarely give step-by-step
   guidance.
3. **They do not integrate** — different focus, terminology and execution guidance.
4. **Used to forecast, not to sell.** Managers apply them to ease forecasting more
   than sellers use them to guide conversations.
5. **Volume-focused, not skills-focused.** They help reps win more deals, not
   handle a customer conversation well.

## SPICED — first-party element definitions (paraphrased)

SPICED keeps the customer's desired impact at the center of every conversation,
and supports both a **consultative** stance (ask questions to understand what the
customer wants, share use cases) and a **provocative** one (as the expert, offer a
different perspective).

| Element | Definition in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Situation** | Facts about the customer that determine whether they fit your ideal customer profile and what is happening in their world — company size, headcount, software used, hiring or security needs, revenue goals |
| **Pain** | The problems the customer buys your solution to solve — e.g., training or recruiting, supporting a global team, passing a security audit, eliminating process errors |
| **Impact** | The results produced by solving the pain; the outcomes the customer is trying to reach. **Emotional for individuals, rational for companies.** |
| **Critical Event** | A specific deadline by which the customer must achieve the impact or suffer negative consequences. It drives the purchase timeline. |
| **Decision** | The people involved, the process they follow, and the criteria they use to evaluate the right solution |

## Critical Event vs. compelling event

The paper draws an explicit distinction: a **Critical Event** has a specific
deadline; a **compelling event** does not.

> **Terminology conflict across sources.** MEDDICC's article on the Compelling
> Event (`meddpicc/compelling-event.md`) defines a compelling event as
> **time-bound**. Winning by Design reserves the deadline for the Critical Event
> and treats a compelling event as having no deadline. The concept both vendors
> value — urgency anchored to a real dated consequence — is the same; the labels
> are not. Both definitions are preserved as published.

## Diagnostic conversation flow (from the paper's diagram, partly legible)

1. **Set up** — open with ACE and an agenda.
2. **Situation** — do your research and talk in context; keep closed questions to
   two or three.
3. **Pain** — if the customer starts venting, ask them to prioritize.
4. **Impact** — ask how it affects their business.
5. **Critical Event** — ask when they need it by, and what happens if they miss
   that date.
6. **Decision criteria**
7. **Summarize** — ask whether you got it right; it resets the conversation.
8. **Empathize** — share a relevant customer story.
9. **Pitch last** — pitching a solution early tends to disengage the customer.

The diagram also flags a common failure: hearing what you wanted to hear, getting
excited, and moving on too soon.

## Key findings

1. Multiple methodologies applied at different stages do not integrate, causing
   confusion and inefficiency.
2. Methodologies get confused with forecasting and are applied to help managers
   forecast rather than to guide sellers.
3. Aligning all revenue capabilities is essential for sustainable recurring growth,
   and **the unifying thread is customer impact**.
